There is a viral quote from 2024 that perfectly captures the current frustration:

"I want AI to do my laundry and dishes so that I can do art and writing, not for AI to do my art and writing so that I can do my laundry and dishes."

For the last three years, AI has been trying to do our art. I believe 2026 will be the year it finally starts doing our laundry.

It will be defined not by the "Knowledge Layer" (what the AI knows) but by the "Action Layer" (what the AI can touch).

Here are my three predictions for how this shift will reshape market research in the coming year.

## Prediction 1: The Rise of the Action Layer (The "Hand")

The real signal for 2026 is coming from features like Anthropic's "Claude Cowork" (research preview) and Meta's acquisition of Manus AI.

The terminology is telling. "Manus" is Latin for "Hand".

We are moving away from the obsession with the "Brain" (pure reasoning capabilities) and towards the "Hand" (execution capabilities). Meta's decision to keep Manus as a standalone tool while integrating its tech signals that Big Tech sees "consumer action" as a primary product, not just a backend feature.

These engines are designed to leave the chat window. They are being built with access to browsers, file systems, and desktop environments. They don't just "know" things; they "touch" things.

Imagine the work that currently makes your research team groan:

- The "Global Report" Nightmare: You have 20 different country reports from local analysts, all saved in slightly different formats with broken chart links. Instead of fixing them one by one, you give an agent access to the folder and the style guide. It opens every file, standardises the taxonomy, aligns the charts, and merges them into a single global master deck.
- The Legacy System Upload: You have a clean dataset in Excel, but it needs to be entered into a rigid, 15-year-old internal database that only accepts manual entry via a slow web portal. The agent takes control of the mouse and keyboard, logging in and entering the data row by row while you work on strategy.

## Prediction 2: The "Authorised Intruder" Redefines Access

As we grant these agents access to our browsers, we open a new frontier for content consumption.

In 2026, agents won't just scrape public web pages. They will act as "authorised intruders," logging into paywalled sites and subscription platforms using your credentials, just as if you were sitting at the screen.

My own tests with tools like Manus have shown they are surprisingly capable of logging into paywalled content with my browser authorisation, all without my physical presence.

This creates massive possibilities for efficiency. An agent could log into five different subscription portals, download the relevant reports, and synthesise them into a briefing document before you even wake up.

But it also creates a massive legal collision.

This capability puts users on a direct collision course with the Terms of Service of almost every major publisher. It forces enterprise security teams to ask uncomfortable questions about who, or what, is actually viewing that confidential screen. It is efficient, but it is legally precarious.

## Prediction 3: The Shift from Operator to Supervisor (For the Brave)

This technological shift implies a professional one. For decades, the analyst was the Operator. We were the hands on the keyboard, the clickers of the mouse, the builders of the pivot table.

In 2026, the leading edge of our industry will become Supervisors.

This isn't a metaphor. With the Action Layer, you will literally assign a task and audit the execution. You might watch a window where a "ghost cursor" navigates the browser, fixing the slide formatting or aligning the text boxes (the end of "pixel pushing").

Your job is no longer to do the clicking. Your job is to audit the logic.

However, this shift won't be overnight.

Let's be realistic. Not every organisation has the appetite, risk tolerance, or technical capability to hand over control of their desktops in 2026. Security compliance alone will be a massive wall for many enterprises.

But the world's early adopters will be there. They will be the ones figuring out how to manage a digital workforce that works alongside the human one.

## The Quality Imperative: A Strong Hand Needs a Sharp Brain

There is a critical nuance here. As we focus on ensuring the "Hand" has access to the right tools, the "Brain" accessed by the hand becomes even more important.

If you point an agent at a low-quality data source, you don't get insight. You get high-speed error.

I've written previously about the risks of the "Slot Machine Effect" (where AI gives inconsistent answers) and the "Digital Yes Man" (where AI tells you what you want to hear). The Action Layer does not solve these problems. In fact, it amplifies them.

An agent that can log into 100 portals in five minutes is useless if it retrieves hallucinated stats or biased summaries. It effectively automates the production of "slop."

This changes the mandate for information providers like Euromonitor International.

We know that our high-quality data and unique insights cannot simply sit locked behind a login screen on Passport. Increasingly, we need to make that intelligence accessible off-platform, specifically for these new AI tools.

This is why my ambition is to push for Model Context Protocol (MCP) access to our content.

We need to move beyond the "Authorized Intruder" model (Prediction 2) and offer a standardised, secure "handshake"—like MCP—that allows your AI agents to retrieve our verified data directly and legally. This turns our data into the reliable fuel for your AI workforce, ensuring that your "Junior Analyst" is building its strategy on bedrock facts, not hallucinations.

## The Price of Admission (and the Doom Loop)

A necessary health warning before we get too excited.

As we hand over the keys to the browser, things will break.

In 2026, we will undoubtedly watch agents get stuck in "doom loops." You will see an AI obsessively click a button that doesn't work, realise it failed, try to fix it, fail again, and consume a large volume of expensive tokens while trying to solve an unfixable problem.

It will be buggy. It won't work every time.

But be patient. The velocity of this industry is unlike anything we have witnessed before. A limitation that feels fatal in January is often a solved problem by March.

My advice for 2026: rarely make a permanent criticism of what AI cannot do. It is only ever what AI cannot do yet.
