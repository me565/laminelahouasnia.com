Let me start with a confession: I spend more of my day 'talking' with AI than with almost anyone else in my life (my wife included, thankfully she understands). I live in Google Gemini, and I have nothing but the utmost respect for tools like Perplexity AI. The technology is a game-changer and I've just gone all in on their new Perplexity Comet browser.

A year ago, the biggest risk of using these tools for market research was the "hallucination"—an answer that was confidently and completely wrong. That problem is largely solved. Today, deep research functionality, powered by more advanced reasoning models, and source citations, make the risk of hallucination far more subtle and, I would argue, far more dangerous.

It's the risk of the "Plausibility Trap": an answer that looks so well-researched on the surface that it feels safe enough for major business decisions.

This isn't just a hypothetical risk; it's a structural problem that industry bodies are formally addressing. The foundational principles being established in the latest revisions to the ICC/ESOMAR International Code, for example, reinforce the principle that researchers have the overall responsibility and oversight for the research they undertake, including when using AI. This underscores a critical truth: you cannot outsource professional accountability to an algorithm.

To see this in action, I put it to the test with a straightforward business question:

"Which countries show the most potential in chocolate confectionery in terms of forecast growth?"

Perplexity's initial answer was rapid and impressive. To push the test further, I ran the same query using a Perplexity Pro search. The result was even more polished, with more sources and deeper qualitative context. Yet, the core problem remained: it was a more beautiful house built on the same foundation of sand.

Then, I asked a specialist market research platform the exact same question. The answer was radically different, and brutally specific.

The fastest-growing markets for 2024-2025 were identified as Estonia (26.8%), Zambia (a modelled market, 26.1%), Latvia (23.8%), and Greece (23.3%).

Now, the crucial point here isn't whether Estonia is a better investment than India. The point is that this answer is verifiable and comparable. It's the start of a real strategic analysis. For instance, the system shows Estonia's growth is driven by a surge in global cocoa prices boosting retail value, even as sales volumes contract—a critical strategic nuance invisible to the generalist tool.

## The Illusion of Research

The public AI's answer, while plausible, was a mirage built on inconsistent data. This is where the trap lies:

- Inconsistent Definitions: The 7.6% CAGR for India and 4.7% for Germany were apples-to-oranges comparisons, pulled from sources with different definitions, baselines, and methodologies. You can't compare them reliably.
- Unreliable and Opaque Sources: The answer was aggregated from a mix of blogs, press releases, and content aggregators. There was no way to verify the underlying data collection methodology or assess the credibility of the original sources.
- Fragmentary Data: The analysis was incomplete, providing specific growth figures for some markets but only vague, qualitative descriptions for others. This makes a true comparative analysis for strategic planning impossible.
- A Void of Business Context: The answer contained data points, but no actual business intelligence. No competitive landscape, no barriers to entry, no pricing dynamics. It gave the what, but completely missed the so what and the now what.

## The Boardroom Test

For those of us in the insights industry, this is where the conversation gets serious. Imagine walking into a boardroom to defend a multi-million-pound market entry strategy. The data you present looks solid, but under the first challenging question about methodology or comparability, it disintegrates. The "Plausibility Trap" doesn't just mislead a strategy; it publicly undermines your credibility. Your professional reputation is at stake.

## The Power of a Closed-Loop System

The answers from accredited market research firms, by contrast, come from a completely different architecture. They don't scrape the web; they interrogate a closed-loop system—a proprietary, global dataset built over years.

Within a system like Euromonitor International's Passport AI:

- "Chocolate Confectionery" is defined the same way across all 100 countries.
- Growth is measured using a consistent metric (Retail Value RSP, y-o-y growth, current prices).
- Every data point is interconnected, allowing for true, like-for-like comparisons.

This is where any specialist market research platform will always outperform a generalist tool. They trade the vastness of the open web for the verifiable integrity of a consistent data universe.
