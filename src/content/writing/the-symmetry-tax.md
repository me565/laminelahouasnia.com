I'll be the first to admit it: I love the Rule of Three.

In communication, it is unbeatable. "Blood, sweat, and tears." "Stop, look, and listen." When we present findings to a client, three strategic pillars are memorable; seventeen are a data dump. I use it constantly to make complex ideas stick.

But there is a massive difference between using the Rule of Three for communication (synthesising the answer) and using it for discovery (finding the answer).

The problem with Gen AI is that it conflates the two.

## The Addiction to Symmetry

If you ask an AI for the drivers of a market trend or the risks of a new launch, it will often default to tidy, symmetrical lists.

This stems from Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). The models are trained to please us, and humans are cognitively biased towards symmetry. We reward answers that look "complete" and "structured."

The AI has learned that a list of three looks like a "correct" answer. A messy list of 8 major factors and 4 minor outliers looks "wrong." So, it optimises for symmetry over truth.

## The Double Danger: Omission and Inflation

This obsession with symmetry creates two distinct risks for market research:

- Omission (The Missing Insight): To fit a complex reality into neat bullet points, the AI deletes the outliers. It removes the messy, weak signals that don't fit the main themes. These outliers are often where the true insight comes from.
- Inflation (The False Insight): This is the more subtle danger. If only two strong factors exist, the AI will often elevate a minor, insignificant point to the same level as the major ones just to complete a set of three. It creates a false equivalence, leading us to waste resources on minor issues because the AI made them look like strategic pillars.

## The "Nested Symmetry" Trap: A Real-World Example

I saw this recently when testing an AI on a famously complex topic: "What are the primary factors that influence purchasing behaviour in China?"

The response was impressive at first glance—comprehensive and detailed. But look closer at the structure.

Under "1. Digital Ecosystem & Platform Dynamics," it broke the drivers down into perfect triads:

- Algorithmic discovery: 1. Short-video content, 2. Trending topics, 3. Influencer recommendations.
- Seamless journeys: 1. Low friction, 2. Impulse-driven shopping, 3. High conversion.

Even when giving a long answer, the AI applied the Rule of Three to tidy up the details.

This structure is plausible. It is symmetrical. It is even factually true at a high level.

But it is reductive. To achieve this perfect micro-symmetry, what did it leave out? Did it merge distinct platform mechanics? Did it delete a fourth, messier factor like the specific role of "private domain traffic" (WeChat groups) because it ruined the rhythm of the list?

By forcing the chaos of Chinese consumer behaviour into neat triplets, it presented a "clean" market view that potentially misses the chaotic edge cases where the real opportunity lies.

## The Solution: Systemic Design, Not Just "Checking"

The answer isn't just "Human-in-the-Loop." Asking a human to manually check every AI summary for missing nuance is unsustainable.

The answer is systemic workflow design. We must design our AI interactions to fight this bias explicitly.

This means treating the AI as a retrieval engine, not a synthesis engine, in the early stages.

- Don't ask: "What are the top 3 drivers?"
- Do ask: "List every potential driver mentioned in the data, regardless of frequency. Do not group them. Prioritise outliers and contradictions."

We need to build workflows that force the AI to "show its working" before it tidies up the room.

As domain experts, our job is to embrace the messiness of human behaviour. We can use the Rule of Three to tell the story at the end, but we can never let the AI use it to write the story at the start.
